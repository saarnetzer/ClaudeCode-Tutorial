Agent squads across five industries — real-world examples

The patterns from Lesson 12 are a skeleton — now we put flesh on it. Five squads from five different worlds: development, content and marketing, data, operations, and customer service. For each: the roles, the chosen pattern and why, and downloadable agent files.

What you'll learn

  • How to translate a problem from any field into a squad design
  • Five detailed squads, from five industries
  • The principles that repeat in all of them — regardless of field
  • The capstone: a squad for your own world
⬇️ Every agent — downloadable Every agent in this lesson's tables is a link to a ready agent file: save it into .claude/agents/ in your project and it's live. You can also grab all 100 agents in one zip. Note: the agent files are currently written in Hebrew — asking Claude to translate one to English is a ten-second task (and good practice).

1. The development world: "the feature squad" 🛠️

The problem: a medium-to-large feature demands planning, implementation, tests and review — and with a single agent that ends in bloated context and declining quality.

The pattern: a pipeline with a stop for human approval after planning (exactly what we built in Lesson 12).

RoleToolsDeliverable (the contract)
planner — plannerread & search only + Write for the SPECSPEC.md: numbered steps + success criterion
coder — implementerfullcode + PROGRESS.md update after every step
test-writer — testerfullpassing tests for the new code
code-reviewer — reviewerread only + git diffa graded findings table

The lesson from this world: the critic must be separate from the implementer — and without write permissions, so the review stays a review.

2. Content and marketing: "the magazine desk" ✍️

The problem: a company blog post demands research, writing, editing and search optimization — four different skills a rushed single writer does averagely.

The pattern: a pipeline, with the editor as a gatekeeper who may send a draft back.

RoleToolsDeliverable (the contract)
researcher — researcherWebSearch, Read, Writeresearch.md: facts + a source for every claim
copywriter — writerRead, Writea draft from research.md only — no invented facts
editor — editorRead, Editan edited version + notes; may send back to the writer
seo-checker — SEO checkerRead, Writea report: titles, description, keywords, internal links

The lesson from this world: the researcher's contract ("a source for every claim") is what keeps the whole squad from inventing facts. Credibility is built at the start of the chain.

3. The data world: "the weekly report squad" 📊

The problem: every week the same business question ("what happened to sales and why") must be answered from raw data — a process of collection, cleaning, analysis and presentation.

The pattern: orchestrator–workers. Why not a pipeline? Because the analysis dictates what follows: an anomaly demands another dive, a normal week does not. The orchestrator decides.

RoleToolsDeliverable (the contract)
collector — collectora database MCP (readonly!)data/raw_week.csv + query documentation
cleaner — cleanerRead, Write, Bash (python)data/clean_week.csv + a log: what was fixed and why
analyst — analystRead, Write, Bash (python)findings.md: 3–5 findings + a confidence level each
visualizer — visualizerRead, Writecharts + an HTML report page

The lesson from this world: two things. One — readonly database permissions (Lesson 7) are what allow the squad to run unsupervised. Two — "a confidence level per finding" in the analyst's contract keeps the report from presenting a guess as a fact.

4. The operations world: "the night shift" 🌙

The problem: incidents do not wait for business hours. Someone must monitor, diagnose, fix what is safe to fix — and wake humans only when truly needed.

The pattern: event-driven orchestrator–workers: runs on a schedule (Lesson 10), and the orchestrator escalates by severity.

RoleToolsDeliverable (the contract)
watcher — watcherRead, Grep (logs), a monitoring MCPa list of anomalies with severity: info / warn / critical
diagnoser — diagnoserread only + limited Basha diagnosis: probable cause + evidence + "safe to auto-fix? yes/no"
fixer — fixerrestricted to an approved fix lista fix + a commit on a branch + verification the incident is gone
reporter — reporterthe send_alert mcp tool (Lesson 11)critical → immediate alert; the rest → a morning digest

The lesson from this world: the "safe to auto-fix?" field in the diagnoser's contract is the entire decision point: the fixer handles only "yes", and every "no" goes to a human. The squad is autonomous exactly up to the risk boundary — and not one step beyond.

5. Commerce and service: "the support squad" 🛒

The problem: an online store inbox gets a mix of product questions, shipping problems, refund requests and complaints — each type needs different expertise and tone.

The pattern: triage → specialists in parallel → human control. The triager splits, each specialist handles its pile simultaneously.

RoleToolsDeliverable (the contract)
triage — triagerRead, Writeper ticket: category + urgency + "needs a human? yes/no"
product-expert — product expertRead (catalog, guides)a draft reply + a source from the docs
orders-expert — orders expertan orders MCP (readonly)a draft reply + the real status from the system
tone-checker — tone checkerRead, Editthe final reply: empathetic, correct, no promises we cannot keep

The lesson from this world: notice what this squad does not do — it does not send replies. The deliverable is drafts in an approval queue, and "needs a human" instantly escalates sensitive complaints. In a world that touches real customers, the squad prepares — the human presses "send". At least until trust is earned, gradually and measurably.

What repeats across all five worlds

  • The same four components: orchestrator, specialists, shared memory, deliverable contracts — only the names change
  • A whole organization = teams of teams: each team stays small and focused, connected through defined deliverables (a PR, a report, a draft) — not "everyone talks to everyone"
  • There is always a critic: a code reviewer, an editor, a tone checker — the review role survives in every field
  • Permissions per role: readonly for readers, approved lists for fixers, sending only through a human
  • A human at the decision points: plan approval, critical escalation, pressing "send" — every squad defines its own

Exercises

Exercise 13.1 Adopt a squad

Pick the squad closest to your world out of the five, and implement a minimal version: three roles, on your real data or code. Run it end to end once.

Trimming tip

You can almost always merge the two middle roles in a first version (writer+editor, cleaner+analyst) — but never give up the separation between doer and critic. It is the first thing that proves its worth.

Exercise 13.2 Design a squad for a new world

Pick a field that did not appear in this lesson — law? education? real estate? music? — and design a squad on paper: the problem, 3–5 roles, tools and permissions for each, deliverable contracts, and the pattern (pipeline / orchestrator–workers / parallel) with reasoning. Then ask Claude to critique the design.

Lightning example: the legal world

A contract-review squad: clause-extractor splits into clauses → risk-flagger marks unusual clauses against the firm checklist → precedent-researcher finds accepted wording → summarizer writes a brief for the attorney. A pipeline; everyone read-only; the deliverable is always a draft for a human attorney — never advice to a client.

Exercise 13.3 Capstone: your squad, live

Take the squad from 13.1 or 13.2 and make it real: full implementation of all roles, launched on a schedule or with one command, running on real tasks for a week. Keep a short journal: what worked, where handoffs broke, what you changed.

The success metric

Not "the squad is perfect" but "the squad is improving": if after a week you have an improvement list born from real runs — you are doing exactly the job of an agent engineer. That is the profession: build, measure, sharpen.

Self-check

1. Which role exists in every successful squad, in every field?

2. What is the iron rule for squads that touch real customers?

3. How do different teams in an organization communicate?
